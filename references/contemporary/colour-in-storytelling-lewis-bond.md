## Why It's Here

The most-watched introduction to narrative colour in film. Not colour science — it is the vocabulary a director, colourist or production designer uses when they say "the colour is doing work." Its two-word framework (**associative** vs **transitional**) is the useful part: it turns "what does red mean" (unanswerable) into "what is red attached to in *this* story, and does it move."

## The Argument, Condensed

**1. Colour predates sound in cinema, and started as spectacle.** Edison and Méliès hand-painted film stock; early colour marked the dream-like, escapist distance of film from reality. Tinting (whole stock dyed one colour) turned it into narrative: Griffith's *Intolerance* (1916) tints each of its four eras differently to separate time and place; Christensen's *Häxan* (1922) plays red (unease) against blue (serenity); von Stroheim's *Greed* (1924) hand-tints the lottery money yellow and lets yellow engulf the whole film as the husband's possessiveness grows. Three-strip Technicolor removed the constraints; the techniques survived unchanged into digital (*Hero*, *Traffic*, *Caligari* → modern cold/gritty grading).

**2. Reactions are innate but meanings are not fixed.** Red is the strongest response, but reads as hate in one film and passion in another. Green is fertility and hope, or the mundane and lifeless, or "the monster" when it's on a person. No rulebook — "an artist's greatest tool is not a degree in psychology, it's their ideas." *Kill Bill*'s yellow works because it evokes hazard, not because yellow "means" anything.

**3. Three dials: hue, saturation, value.** Change any one and you have a different film. (He uses HSV; the point survives translation to LCH.) Cites the Man of Steel desaturation comparison: same footage, different movie.

**4. Schemes serve two purposes: balance or discordance.**
- *Balance* — analogous (Moonrise Kingdom: greens/browns/yellows, calming, nostalgic), complementary (red/green, blue/orange, yellow/purple), triadic (Pierrot le Fou: playful, makes an improvised world believable). Balance is not the same as pleasant: *Apocalypse Now*'s Kurtz compound is balanced, and the orange mist reads as toxic.
- *Discordance* — one saturated colour that doesn't fit gives the eye a focus or resting point and pulls attention (why game design paints important objects in bold saturated colour: *Mirror's Edge*). Introducing a new colour into an established scheme signals that the mood has been unsettled. "The audience will notice that which doesn't fit in."

**5. Colour is context.** Gene Hackman's pale pink in *The Royal Tenenbaums* tells you not to take his cruelty seriously; *Grand Budapest*'s candy palette says nothing here carries much weight. Lightsaber colours tell you who's good before anyone speaks.

**6. Two jobs: associative and transitional.**
- **Associative:** a consistently repeated colour binds to a subject or idea. *We Need to Talk About Kevin* shows almost no violence, but red is everywhere, so violence is the undercurrent. *The Godfather* makes orange mean death — associations need not follow social convention. Rule of reading: if a colour keeps recurring, find the subject it is attached to.
- **Transitional:** once a colour is bound to a subject, a shift in that colour marks a change in the subject — a location, or a state of mind. *The Last Emperor* moves red → orange → yellow → green as Puyi learns about the world: consecutive hues, so character and colour wheel have both turned 180°. *Malcolm X* shifts gradually across the arc; a threat can shift the palette instantaneously.
- Combined: *Blue Is the Warmest Colour* — blue is associative (Adèle's freedom and the relationship, and love is blue, not red), and its saturation is transitional: vivid at the height of the affair, pale as love fades, finally one blue dress in a world of greys. *Vertigo* — red for Scottie's dangerous passion, green for Madeleine; as Judy is remade, green re-enters until the hotel neon engulfs her.

**7. Attaching colour to a character.** Costume is the default because the character is always seen with it (*Reservoir Dogs*' names). *I Am Love* swaps two characters' clothes between scenes to hint at their relationship. *Breaking Bad* tracks Walt's moral slide through his wardrobe: red, then a darker shirt revealed beneath — "something dark lies beneath the surface."

Closes on Roger Deakins: it is easy to make colour look good, hard to make it serve a story.

## How to Use This in the Skill

- When asked "what does colour X mean in film," answer with association + transition, not a symbolism table. Ask: what subject is the colour attached to, and does it move across the story?
- Discordance is the film-grammar name for the "accent colour" in palette design: one saturated outlier on a balanced base is a focal device, and adding a new hue to a settled scheme reads as disruption.
- The three-dial framing (hue / saturation / value) is the right entry point for non-technical readers; translate to OKLCH L/C/h once they need to construct rather than read.
- The historical thread (hand-colouring → tinting → Technicolor) connects to `historical/` material on early colour processes.
